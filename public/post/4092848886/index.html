📰 The $31 Trillion Hangover The numbers say “prosperity.” The lives say something else. Growth is solid, unemployment is low, stocks are near records. Yet people like Leith Olson — on a combined income of around $150,000 — are cutting camps for their kids, skipping games, and driving decade‑old Toyotas. The mid‑century promise of the American middle class has been quietly repriced. Over the past several years, the cost of living has jumped close to 30 percent while wages have finally slipped back below inflation. Total worker compensation has barely moved since 2025, even as corporate profits sit at their fattest share of national income since the early 1950s. The president calls the affordability crisis a hoax. People experiencing it call it their life. This is not a story about the poor versus the rich. It is a story about people doing “everything right” and watching the system move the goalposts. A veteran municipal worker in Durham cuts meat and snacks to pay for gas just to get to his job. A state hospital employee sees a 3 percent raise swallowed by health costs and inflation. A cafeteria worker organizes a union because “efficiency” at the bank means squeezing older staff while child care for a special‑needs kid is so expensive it may as well not exist. Both parties are in the line of fire, and not in a heroic way. Republicans sell a strong economy while their own voters talk about “corruption” and “irresponsible capitalists.” Democrats promise to “fix it” once they win, but struggle to explain what that actually means for someone staring at rent, insurance and grocery bills. The economy feels less like a social contract and more like a maze: you can get ahead, but, as one worker put it, “you gotta be devious in how you do it.” The irony is brutal: consumer spending still props up the system, fueled by shrinking savings and one‑off tax refunds. People are keeping the show running by quietly burning their own future. On paper, the American dream remains available.
