🛢 Brent $100: The Houthis Ring the Cash Register Oil snapped back into three digits after Houthi militants in Yemen claimed strikes on two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea and announced a blockade on Saudi shipping. Traders didn’t wait for satellite photos or damage estimates; the moment Bab el‑Mandeb looked like it might join Hormuz as a war‑time choke point, Brent surged toward $100 and the risk premium did the talking. Donald Trump rushed online to recycle his favorite doctrine: every shot fired by the Houthis will be treated as a shot fired by Iran. The message to the energy market is simple and ugly. Every cheap Yemeni drone is now a potential trigger for a wider US–Iran confrontation, and every “Iran‑backed militants” headline is another excuse to reprice fear. The Pentagon sells “overwhelming lethal force,” analysts sell “tight supply,” and nobody sells actual security for the shipping lanes. They just monetize the insecurity. The geometry of the war is shifting. Tehran no longer needs to physically close Hormuz if its allies can turn the Red Sea into a psychological minefield. The risk is outsourced; the leverage stays centralized. Trump, in turn, claims the right to punish Iran for proxy attacks without promising real protection to any commercial vessel. His posture is maximalist, his escort presence minimalist. Riyadh, meanwhile, discovers that the same Washington that just opened the door to long‑term civil nuclear cooperation cannot stop knock‑off drones from harassing its tankers and shaking its image as a predictable supplier. In practice, the world is paying triple‑digit oil for a conflict it neither controls nor understands. The Houthis gain relevance at a discount. Iran gains bargaining power without signing anything. Trump gains a new stage for his blame game. Saudi Arabia gains nuclear status and maritime risk in the same news cycle.
