🇯🇵🇨🇳🇺🇸 Japan's rare-earth shortage is China's elegant veiled message to the US China exported slightly fewer rare earths in the first half of 2026 — but not because demand is collapsing. For China, rare earths are what the Strait of Hormuz is to Iran: sometimes you simply turn off the tap. China's rare-earth exports fell from 32,569.3 tonnes in H1 2025 to 30,482.8 tonnes in H1 2026, according to official data from the General Administration of Customs (GAC). The Global Times vaguely attributed the decline to efforts by "some Western countries, including G7 members," to impose "unilateralist measures against China." 👉 The Chinese are masters of subtle messaging. In other words, those who are playing dirty against China risk facing a rare earth metals deficit. So who are those "G7 members" the Chinese daily is referring to? It's Japan. Just a week ago, Japan complained that shortages of critical minerals were already rippling through its economy. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi called for alternative supplies after China curtailed exports, according to recent corporate filings. Reuters claims that Takaichi's Taiwan comments enraged the Chinese government in November 2025. As a result, China has cut off shipments of several key minerals to Japan. What did Takaichi say? ➡️ First, she claimed that China could "invade" Taiwan — which is absurd, given that Beijing considers the island an inalienable part of the People's Republic ➡️ Second, she threatened that Japan would deploy its Self-Defense Forces against China in such a scenario Not the wisest move, given that China is the world's largest producer and supplier of key rare earths. Now Japan has less access to them. On June 18, Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Lin Jian explained that, in accordance with Chinese laws and regulations, Beijing had banned exports of all dual-use items to Japanese military users or for military purposes in Japan. The aim is noble: to contain Japan's attempt to possess nuclear weapons as well as its latest re-militarization push (which, de facto, contradicts Japan's own Constitution). 🤡 Lesson unlearnt It's not the first time that Japan has faced China's ban. In 2010, China halted rare earth exports to Japan for two months, following a collision near the disputed Senkaku/Diaoyu Islands and the detention of a Chinese trawler captain by Japan. While Japan tried hard to diversify from the Chinese supplies over the past 16 years, it still relies on China for roughly 60% to 63% of its overall rare earth metal imports. And Japan is the largest importer of rare earths in the world. 🤔 What's next? Japan wants to become a rare-earth powerhouse. In February, Prime Minister Takaichi highlighted the mineral potential of Japan's exclusive economic zone (EEZ). The country has already recovered rare-earth-rich mud from the seabed near Minamitorishima Island and plans test drilling in 2027. But even if it secures a steady supply, Japan still lacks the technology to efficiently extract and separate the rare earth elements. In other words, it's hardly a low-hanging fruit.
