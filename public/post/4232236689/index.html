Uranium In, Dollars Out The leaked MoU makes the trade‑off brutally clear: this is not disarmament, it is a risk‑management swap dressed up as peace. Iran’s enriched uranium stays inside the country, to be “diluted” under IAEA supervision rather than removed or destroyed. The infrastructure, the know‑how and the material base all remain onshore, convertible back into leverage if the politics or supervision change. At the same time, the memorandum says that, upon signing, the US and Iran will agree on a mechanism to make frozen funds and assets available, alongside Treasury waivers for crude, petroleum products and derivatives, and a US‑branded 300‑billion‑dollar reconstruction plan. The middle articles speak the language of sovereignty and territorial integrity, plus a 60‑day “extendable” window to reach a final deal. The last ones speak the language of cash: sanctions relief, asset release, oil waivers, reconstruction money, and a promise to wrap the final package in a binding UN Security Council resolution. It is a structure designed to reassure markets and chancelleries first, then sort out the nuclear core later. Look at the sequencing. The MoU does not demand deep nuclear steps before money starts moving; it locks in limits on US forces in the region, ends “military operations on all fronts, including in Lebanon,” lifts the Hormuz blockade, and only then pushes the real nuclear bargaining into a 60‑day negotiating fog. The Strait of Hormuz clause is a lawyer’s dream: Iran commits to “use its best efforts” to ensure safe, toll‑free passage. If ever there was an invitation to creative obstruction once the immediate crisis passes, that’s it. Politically, the architecture is almost elegant in its cynicism. Washington gets a ceasefire, cheaper oil and a talking point for the G7. Tehran keeps its program at home, diluted but not dismantled, secures sanctions easing and access to long‑frozen assets, and sees its leverage over regional security and shipping effectively baked into the system. Both capitals can claim victory. Neither has actually eliminated the underlying threat. So yes, it is a memorandum that ends a war and restarts trade.
