🇺🇸💣 Eleven Nights, a Thousand Strikes, and One Strait That Won’t Budge CENTCOM says it has completed the 11th consecutive night of US strikes on Iran, hitting “military operation centers, maritime capabilities, aircraft hangars, drone storage facilities and military logistics infrastructure” to “further degrade” Iran’s ability to threaten shipping in the Strait of Hormuz. On paper, it sounds decisive; in practice, it looks like a campaign stuck in its own loop. The mission is narrow: make it harder for Iran to hit ships. Most of the fire is aimed at sea‑denial infrastructure — coastal defense systems, naval facilities, anti‑ship missile and drone sites, surveillance networks and logistics hubs along the Gulf and Gulf of Oman — with recent inland hits in Tabriz, Shiraz and Semnan. Hardened complexes deeper in the country, tied to nuclear and long‑range missile programs, remain largely untouched because they sit outside that target set. CENTCOM’s own numbers are heavy. Recent waves hit over 140 targets in one night and more than 300 across a week. Since May, US forces say they’ve helped hundreds of commercial vessels and hundreds of millions of barrels of crude transit Hormuz while Iran has attacked more than 30 civilian ships in the same corridor. Traffic continues under escort and under fire — which is exactly what “keeping the strait open” means in this war. The real problem is strategic: after eleven nights — and, by broader Pentagon counts, thousands of targets hit since the war began — there is still no visible plan for how this confrontation ends. Iran rejects any deal that dilutes its leverage over Hormuz and keeps enforcing that claim with force. Washington speaks about degrading capabilities and protecting commerce, but offers no pathway to a mutually accepted regime for the sea lane or to a settlement that makes strikes unnecessary. Making it messier, the US has itself adopted a blockade‑style posture: escorting some ships, stopping or turning back others, and bombing coastal infrastructure, while presenting this as a defense of “free passage.” Hormuz is open enough for trade to move, dangerous enough to keep risk and prices high, and contested enough for both Tehran and Washington to claim they’re the ones safeguarding it. The strikes clearly reduce the ease and tempo of Iranian attacks.
