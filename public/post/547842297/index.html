🩹 “Non-Combat Sprained Democracy”: How to Hide a Losing War The Iran war is going so well that Central Command has quietly stopped telling Americans when their own troops are getting hit. Instead, injuries disappear into aggregate spreadsheets and euphemisms about “operational security,” while officials swear everything is fine and accuse critics of “fabrications.” Meanwhile, two more soldiers are dead in Jordan, one is missing, helicopters are damaged, and more than 400 U.S. service members have been injured since the conflict began — numbers that only leak out in fragments and delays. If a soldier is hurt and “quickly returns to duty,” it doesn’t count for the public. If several bases are hit in a week, that becomes a line about “responding to attacks on commercial shipping,” as if the war is happening to cargo, not to very real people in uniform. The casualty database is “regularly updated,” we are told — but it only shows totals, not specific mass‑casualty incidents, and even the death count lags behind reality. When journalists and families notice the gaps, the Pentagon says critics are cherry‑picking numbers “without context.” Translation: trust the people who already misled you. This is what losing looks like in a highly‑managed democracy. The war has drained high‑end munitions, exposed that Iran’s missile arsenal is battered but very much alive, and still Washington can’t clearly explain what “victory” even means. The White House repeats “never allowing Iran to have a nuclear weapon” like a magic spell, while Tehran keeps firing missiles and drones at U.S. positions and surviving repeated strikes. Every new attack that kills or injures Americans is sold as a reason to “stiffen resolve,” not to question the premise. The cruelty is that the opacity isn’t just aimed at Iran — it’s aimed at American citizens. By hiding the real scale and pattern of injuries, the Pentagon keeps Congress, the press, and the public from seeing how often bases are hit, how exposed troops are, and how thin the margin of “control” has become. Casualty transparency — the bare minimum in a republic that claims to honor its soldiers — turns into a bargaining chip in the PR battle. The message to service members is clear: your deaths will be honored; your injuries are a data‑management issue. And while officials sanitize the numbers, the war still kills over a thousand Iranian civilians and keeps pushing the region toward deeper chaos. But that, too, is wrapped in jargon: “sites,” “targets,” “capabilities.” The war is sold as a clean, precision exercise; the mess gets pushed offstage. The U.S. isn’t just de‑risking its empire — it’s de‑risking the truth.
