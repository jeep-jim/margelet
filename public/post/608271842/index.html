Trump’s ICE Assault Cases: Built for Intimidation, Not Convictions Trump’s immigration war runs on a simple move: agents yell “18 U.S.C. 111!” while smashing windows, tackling protesters and dragging immigrants out of cars — then call it “assault on a federal officer.” The data now shows how thin that is. More than 550 people have been charged under that statute in ICE and Border Patrol operations. Of over 400 resolved cases, nearly half collapsed: juries acquitted, judges tossed charges, or prosecutors backed off. In normal federal criminal practice, the government wins more than 90 percent of the time; here, it’s closer to a coin flip. What kills these cases isn’t clever lawyering, it’s video. Again and again, clips show agents as the first to use force — shoving, tackling, pepper‑spraying, smashing car windows — then charging whoever screamed or resisted. Judges have caught agents destroying or hiding evidence, from repairing damaged government cars before defense experts could inspect them to making a young woman delete photos from her phone, including the “Recently Deleted” folder. At least two judges ruled evidence was deliberately destroyed, violating defendants’ rights. The statute itself requires forcible assault or interference. Biden’s DOJ used it against Jan. 6 rioters and didn’t lose a case until Trump returned and pardoned them. Under Trump, ICE and Border Patrol turned it into a general‑purpose weapon against protest and observation: people were charged for filming, following, honking, blowing whistles, or yelling “La migra is coming,” with no physical contact alleged. In more than 100 cases, prosecutors never claimed any agent was hurt; in others, injuries came from officers’ own actions, like glass from windows they broke themselves. The stories are variations on one theme. Two mothers in Charlotte try to warn neighbors about an ICE sweep, end up trapped on a dead‑end street, screaming into a 911 call as a rifle barrel smashes their SUV window. They’re charged with “impeding,” celebrated online as “excellent arrests,” then quietly cleared when the U.S. attorney drops the case. A school staffer in Minneapolis is shoved, tackled and choked for moving toward a crowd watching an arrest; his “assault” charge is first reduced, then abandoned when video doesn’t match the official story. An undocumented Honduran in Laredo, barely five feet tall, is prosecuted for punching a much larger officer; body‑cam shows the officer grabbing him by the neck, forcing him down and punching him. The jury acquits; he’s slated for deportation anyway. Officials claim they’re facing a “massive increase” in threats and are right to “prioritize” these prosecutions. The record suggests something else: a law repurposed as a fear lever, a dragnet for anyone who gets near immigration raids, and a trail of discarded cases and judicial opinions describing ICE and DOJ behavior as “flagrant,” “in bad faith,” and “shocking to the universal sense of justice.” Yes, some people really assaulted agents, and many others took plea deals. But when almost every contested case breaks under basic scrutiny, you’re not seeing law and order.
