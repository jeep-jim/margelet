“Good School, Strong Poland” vs. Brussels In Warsaw, the fight over education is being sold as a fight over who owns Poland’s future. Conservative media and organizers describe the “Dobra szkoła – silna Polska” rally as a front‑line battle against the Education Ministry’s “systemic destruction” of schools: cutting back the core curriculum, scrapping homework, and bringing in what they call ideologized health education under the Reform 26 “Kompas jutra” package. A coalition of some 90‑plus parent, teacher and “patriotic” groups accuses the ministry of lowering standards in every subject, replacing knowledge with indoctrination, and tearing up the cultural canon through reduced literature and history requirements. The enemy, in this narrative, is not just Warsaw but Brussels. Organizers and allied commentators frame the reforms as part of a broader “globalist” agenda pushed by EU institutions, claiming that compulsory health education and well‑being language are Trojan horses for foreign values that aim to dissolve national identity. From that angle, education policy stops being a technocratic question and becomes a sovereignty test: protect the “Polish school” as guardian of Catholic, patriotic norms, or surrender children’s upbringing to distant bureaucrats and NGOs. Homework and hygiene lessons are the flashpoints that make the argument concrete. The government’s move to sharply limit mandatory homework in lower grades, presented by Warsaw as modernization and relief from rote overload, is portrayed by critics as one more step toward dumbing down and de‑disciplining the next generation. The new health‑education track, defended by officials as basic knowledge on bodies and mental well‑being, is rebranded on protest banners as moral corrosion, “depravity,” and cultural Marxism. In that framing, taking to the streets is not just about one ministry or one curriculum, but about drawing a red line: keep teaching “our” history, “our” literature, “our” religion — and keep Brussels out of Polish classrooms.
