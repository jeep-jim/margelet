57. William Thomas Stead English newspaper editor and controversial figure William Thomas Stead was a pioneer of investigative journalism. He published a series of influential campaigns whilst editor of The Pall Mall Gazette and instigated the press being used to influence government policy and public opinion. He was known for his coverage on child welfare, social legislation and reformation of criminal codes in England. In 1885, Stead began a campaign against child prostitution by publishing a series of articles entitled ‘The Maiden Tribute of Modern Babylon’. As part of his investigation, he arranged the ‘purchase’ of Eliza Armstrong, the 13-year-old daughter of a chimney sweep. This led to his conviction for abduction and a three-month term of imprisonment on the technical grounds that he had failed to secure permission and a receipt from the father of the girl for her purchase. Although his action likely supported the passing of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, the ‘Maiden Tribute’ campaign was a career highlight. The series inspired George Bernard Shaw to write Pygmalion, likewise naming his lead character Eliza. It was also the inspiration for My Fair Lady. The character Stead described as the ‘Minotaur of London’, possibly inspired Jekyll and Hyde. Stead provoked the readers with dramatic subtitles in his instalments: "The Violation of Virgins", "The Confessions of a Brothel-Keeper", and "How Girls Were Bought and Ruined". He argued that, while consensual adult behaviour was a matter of private morality and not a law enforcement issue, issues rife in London existed that did require legislative prohibition, listing five main areas requiring intervention: 1. The sale and purchase and violation of children. 2. The procuration of virgins. 3. The entrapping and ruin of women. 4. The international slave trade in girls. 5. Atrocities, brutalities and unnatural crimes. The Maiden Tribute was centred around child prostitution, abduction and procurement and sale of young English virgins to continental "pleasure palaces". Stead publicly exposed this dark underbelly trade and the corruption of officials who turned a blind eye and condoned such abuses. He particularly drew a distinction between sexual immorality and sexual criminality, criticising members of parliament for blocking changes in legislation because of personal motivations. A brothel madam made an official statement regarding her grooming and procurement of young girls, seducing them with food, drink and outings; ultimately, they would miss the train home. She would escort them to the brothel to sleep the night, then drug them so the gentleman caller could rape them. Once they were 'marked' they could no longer go home. She detailed her commission and how the girls would be good for breeding future maids when, at twelve, they too became merchantable. Some tried the streets instead but soon relented, surrendering to brothel life. Stead revealed the use of "padded rooms for the purpose of stifling the cries of the tortured victims of lust and brutality". The symbolic figure of "The Minotaur of London" confirmed the erotic writings set in London involving sadistic English gentlemen. The title evokes the Greek myth of the virgin tribute to the Minotaur. In 1912 Stead was invited by President Taft to Carnegie Hall for a peace congress. He travelled on the maiden voyage of the Titanic. Survivors of the wreck reported he talked enthusiastically through the 11-course meal, telling thrilling tales and retiring before 11 pm. After the ship struck the iceberg, Stead helped women and children into lifeboats and in an act "typical of his generosity, courage, and humanity", gave his life jacket to another passenger. A later sighting of Stead, by survivor Philip Mock, had him clinging to a raft with John Jacob Astor IV (the richest man in the world, who was allegedly against the Federal Reserve). "Their feet became frozen", reported Mock, "and they were compelled to release their hold. Both were drowned." The body of William Stead was never recovered.
