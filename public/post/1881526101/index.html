TPS: Temporary Protected Status, Permanently Disposable People The Supreme Court just handed Donald Trump a green light to rip the floor out from under hundreds of thousands of people it once invited to stand on it. In a 6–3 decision, the conservative majority said the administration can end Temporary Protected Status for Haitians and Syrians, clearing the way to strip legal status from roughly 350,000 Haitians and about 6,100 Syrians who have been living and working in the United States under a program Congress created in 1990 for people fleeing war, disaster and collapse. Samuel Alito’s opinion is a lawyer’s dream and a migrant’s nightmare. He leans on the statute’s magic words — “no judicial review of any determination” — to tell federal courts they are basically not allowed to question the Homeland Security secretary’s decision to terminate TPS, no matter how flimsy the process, so long as the administration can articulate race‑neutral reasons on paper. The Court goes further and waves away claims of racial animus toward Haitians, despite a record full of Trump’s rants about Haitians coming from a “filthy, dirty, disgusting” country and his bogus campaign line about Haitians in Ohio eating people’s pets; Elena Kagan’s dissent spells it out bluntly: the statements “shout” racial motive, but the majority plugs its ears. For Trump, this is the immigration jackpot he’s been chasing since getting back into office. His team has already moved to end TPS for 13 of the 17 countries that had it when Biden left, throttled refugee resettlement, and slowed asylum processing to a crawl; now he can start turning long‑settled engineers, nurses, students and caregivers into deportable “illegals” with the stroke of a pen. Many of these people have no current deportation orders, U.S.‑born kids, mortgages and decades of ties; the Court’s ruling means their status can evaporate almost overnight, with their legal right to challenge the policy chopped down to a narrow, uphill constitutional claim. Both sides in the case agreed on one brutal premise: TPS is, on paper, temporary, and once a designation ends, its recipients have no statutory right to stay. What immigrant‑rights lawyers argued is that Homeland Security didn’t even pretend to seriously review conditions in Haiti and Syria, as the law requires, and instead followed a preordained script to terminate as many countries as possible. Lower courts agreed and froze the terminations, calling out the sham process — until the Supreme Court stepped in, fast‑tracked the case, and told judges to stand down because Congress wrote the statute to make the secretary’s “determination” effectively unreviewable. This isn’t just about Haitians and Syrians. The same logic clears the path for Trump to yank protections from TPS holders from a dozen other countries — including Venezuela, Honduras, Nepal and Afghanistan — with minimal fear of judicial pushback. It tightens a broader architecture where the executive can close doors on migrants from “unsafe” countries while still insisting, for domestic consumption, that America remains a beacon of refuge; the Statue of Liberty stays on postcards while the fine print in the Federal Register says “status subject to termination without notice, no returns, no refunds.” The Court didn’t just hand Trump a legal win; it handed every future president a loaded precedent.
