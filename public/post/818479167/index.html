Gulf Protection: Warranty Expired The deal with Iran may reopen Hormuz, but it doesn’t reopen faith in the American shield. Gulf states just watched a “U.S.-backed security architecture” fail its live test. For almost four months, Iran and its allies hit bases, energy facilities and even hotels across Bahrain, Qatar, the U.A.E., Kuwait and beyond, while Washington’s famous umbrella leaked drones and missiles through the gaps. The superpower that promised to keep the shipping lanes open also misread Iran’s ability to close them — and needed a framework deal just to crawl back to the prewar status quo. Now the repair bill is political. Gulf elites are asking themselves what everyone else was already whispering: if this is what protection looks like, what exactly are we paying for. Analysts say the U.S. security guarantee “is no longer reliable in the way they thought it was,” and Gulf officials are already talking, quietly, about reducing dependence. But even their Plan B is cautious to the point of self‑parody: diversify ties, yes, but only with partners Washington can live with, because thousands of American troops and bases on their soil still set the outer limits of their sovereignty. Inside the region, a different comparison hurts more. Iran, under massive sanctions, still managed to build and field its own drones, missiles and defense infrastructure. Gulf states, drowning in cash and Western hardware, largely outsourced their security thinking to others. Kuwaiti scholars now say out loud what rulers preferred to leave implicit: if Iran can build a national defense under pressure, the monarchies can too — they just chose to buy systems instead of capabilities, and status instead of strategy. The obvious answer is regional: cooperate, pool procurement, stop treating every neighbor as an arms‑race rival. On paper, that sounds reasonable. In practice, it threatens the political economy of the Gulf — parallel militaries, overlapping contracts, and leaders who like their toys bespoke, not standardized. So the post‑deal landscape looks like this: Washington declares a “great peace,” Tehran advertises strategic resilience, and the Gulf discovers that being a client doesn’t mean being safe.
