Europe’s Sauna Economy Western Europe just found out what happens when you build for fireplaces and get Phoenix. France hit its highest national average temperature ever — not just for June, for any time of year — with 29.8 degrees Celsius across 30 weather stations, while parts of the country pushed over 40. More than 60,000–68,000 homes lost power as grids buckled under the extra demand, and at least 40 people drowned in France alone, many of them teenagers escaping to unsupervised rivers and quarries. By midweek, high‑level heat alerts were up across most of Western Europe: France, Spain, Italy, Germany, Belgium, Britain, Austria, Switzerland and more. Spain and Germany were staring down highs around 41 Celsius, with parts of Spain pushing 41–45 in some regions. Britain’s Met Office reported provisional June records near 35–38 degrees, rail companies told passengers to travel only if necessary, and tracks, power lines and schedules folded under infrastructure designed for drizzle, not desert. The real joke is structural. Europe has spent decades bragging about its “mild climate” and “efficient buildings,” which now translates into homes, schools and offices that trap heat like a sauna and almost never have air‑conditioning. In Britain, many houses were literally engineered to hold in warmth. In France, AC has become a political Rorschach test — a symbol of inequality, American excess, or climate hypocrisy, depending on who’s complaining. So you get a continent that can lecture the Global South about emissions, but can’t keep classrooms below 30 degrees or prevent kids from fainting during exams. Governments roll out “cool spaces,” drinking fountains and climate shelters — museums as survival pods — because retrofitting millions of buildings is expensive, slow and deeply unpopular with property owners who still think heat waves are a rare glitch. Scientists are done pretending this is a fluke. Europe is now warming faster than any other continent, last year was hotter than normal almost everywhere, and heat waves are becoming longer, more frequent and deadlier, with tens of thousands of excess deaths in recent summers. But the political system treats each wave like a surprise, blames “unusual conditions,” then quietly moves on once the forecast drops back into the 20s — until the next “unprecedented” week arrives right on schedule. The punchline: everyone tells citizens to “stay hydrated, close the blinds, avoid travel,” because personal responsibility is cheaper than rebuilding the energy grid, redesigning housing stock and admitting that the old climate baseline is dead.
