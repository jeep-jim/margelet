🛢 Brent at $100: Two Chokepoints, One Bad Bet Brent just punched through $100 a barrel after at least one Saudi tanker was hit in the Red Sea and the Houthis declared they’re now running a private blockade service for Riyadh’s oil. Hormuz is already half‑shut by Iranian attacks and mines; now Bab el‑Mandeb is on the board too, and the market finally understands that “energy security” is just a line in speeches. The Houthis, Iran‑aligned and bored of sitting out the war, say they hit two Saudi vessels, Encelia and Layla, to enforce their new ban on Saudi shipping. Ship‑tracking shows Encelia suddenly veering off course near the Farasan islands, then broadcasting “not under command” and freezing within reach of Yemen’s coast, with Saudi authorities reporting a fire at the bow but no casualties. Lloyd’s and other monitors can confirm one hit, not a double strike — but the price doesn’t care. Brent is up roughly 40 percent this month already; one more route under threat and it rockets into three‑digit territory. Trump responds with the usual sermon: Iran will be held directly responsible for any further Houthi attacks, “major military punishment” will follow, and he’s “very disappointed” in the rebels who had, until now, “acted very professionally and smart.” Marco Rubio, speaking from an ASEAN meeting that is now basically an oil‑panic summit, warns the Houthis they’ve been “suckered into this by the Iranians,” calls them “smart” for staying out at first, then notes that one of the ships was Chinese‑flagged — meaning they just poked Beijing as well. Washington wants Iran to feel the heat for every cheap drone launched from Yemen, but it also needs Asian importers calm enough not to dump the whole war back on the White House. On the water, the geometry is brutal. Hormuz used to carry around a fifth of global oil and gas flows; that artery has been choked for months by Iranian attacks and mines, with US–Israeli strikes turning the area into a live‑fire zone. Now a second narrow throat, Bab el‑Mandeb, which handles about 7 percent of global oil, is threatened by a self‑appointed blockade that has already forced multiple ships to turn around before the first confirmed hit. “Double blockade” is how Lloyd’s describes the scenario for Asian importers; it’s a nice phrase for what is essentially a hostage situation built on two shallow channels and one widening war. Iran discovers it doesn’t have to close Hormuz completely if its friends can spook the Red Sea from Yemen. The risk is outsourced, the leverage stays at home. Trump discovers he can threaten “major punishment” for every naval incident without promising actual protection for the tankers — doctrine as content, deterrence as a press release. The Houthis discover that for the cost of a handful of drones and missiles, they can move from local insurgents to global price‑makers, with their blockade announcement echoed by ship‑tracking data and satellite imagery instead of slogans alone. The rest of the world, especially in Southeast Asia, discovers what “deep concern” really buys: communiqués at ASEAN, rising fuel bills, and no control over the strikes around Hormuz and the Red Sea.
