Kazan: The Colonies Talk Back While the West is still busy lecturing the “Global South” about values, Putin is in Kazan doing something much more dangerous to the old order: treating them like partners, not plantations. At the ASEAN–Russia summit, he sits down with the prime ministers of Thailand and Singapore and talks trade, energy, and “regional cooperation” — but the subtext is clear: the days when Southeast Asia’s role was to feed Western supply chains and swallow Western crises are over. The agenda is wrapped in polite language: Middle East tensions, global energy security, supply‑chain shocks. In practice, it’s a live demo of a new wiring diagram. Instead of oil flows and shipping routes running only through Western-controlled hubs, Moscow offers discounted energy, nuclear tech, food security deals, and logistics corridors that bypass the old colonial choke points. The message to ASEAN is simple: you don’t have to beg Brussels or Washington for stability when you can arbitrage between powers that suddenly need you more than you need them. For Russia, this is more than sanctions survival. It’s revenge on a system where the “rules-based order” meant London, Washington and Brussels write the rules while the Global South provides bodies, bases and commodities. Every handshake in Kazan says: that monopoly is broken. The same West that spent decades turning Asia into a cheap-labor assembly line and a buffer zone now watches its former “periphery” sign long-term energy and security frameworks with a country it tried to erase from the global economy. Of course, Moscow isn’t a charity; it’s another empire with its own appetite. But the symbolism bites. ASEAN leaders sit in a Russian city, in their own right, negotiating five‑year roadmaps on energy and trade without asking the IMF for permission or the State Department for a waiver. The old colonial script — metropole dictates, South obeys — is being rewritten live, and Putin’s bet is that once the Global South gets used to pricing its future across multiple centers, the West will never get its monopoly back. That’s why Kazan matters more than any communique: it’s not a summit, it’s a crack in the old command chain.
