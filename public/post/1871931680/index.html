The Iran War’s “New Normal” The war didn’t just hit oil; it rewired how the global economy thinks about risk, energy and American power. The first shock is structural. A near‑shutdown of oil and gas flows from the Gulf and a price spike on top of the 2022 shock forced importers in Europe and Asia to confront how exposed they are to a few chokepoints and a handful of producers. Some, like Japan and South Korea, slid back into dirtier fuels such as coal to keep the lights on, but the deeper response is acceleration: more renewables, more nuclear, more diversification of suppliers. Wind and solar have already reached the point where they can outcompete gas on cost in many markets, and investors now see renewables as projects with payback periods measured in a couple of years instead of decades. The second shift is geopolitical. The war strained relations inside the oil club, with the U.A.E. walking out of OPEC+ and Saudi Arabia edging closer to Russia, just as sanctions on Moscow were loosened enough to let it cash in on higher prices. At the same time, Latin American producers from Brazil to Guyana are racing to expand capacity to capture demand from buyers trying to insure against Middle East instability. Overlaying all of this is China, which already dominates manufacturing of solar panels, turbines, grids and batteries; every crisis‑driven renewable build‑out outside the Middle East quietly increases Beijing’s leverage over other countries’ energy security. Trust is the other casualty. Iran has shown that it can close or tax Hormuz at will, turning a theoretical risk into a practical tool that permanently raises insurance premiums and freight costs. Gulf financial and tourist hubs that sold themselves as safe, frictionless platforms now carry the memory of missile strikes on LNG plants, petrochemical complexes, hotels and data centers. And the United States, which spent decades presenting its Navy as guarantor of free navigation, has just demonstrated that even its presence cannot ensure uninterrupted passage in a contested strait. For investors and policymakers, that translates into a lasting “risk surcharge” on anything tied to the region. Macro‑economically, the war hit just as the world was beginning to exhale. Before the first strikes, global forecasters were preparing to upgrade growth and celebrate fading inflation. Instead, they are now cutting growth projections, watching inflation re‑accelerate and assuming higher interest rates for longer. Governments already burdened with large debts are spending more on interest, energy subsidies and defense, leaving less room for long‑term investment. Asian economies, hit hardest by supply shocks, are back at development banks’ doors for emergency support.
