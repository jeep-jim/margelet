Israel's Democracy: Where Everyone Agrees the Economy Matters and Nobody Votes On It A new poll just handed Israeli politics its own confession booth, and the testimony is glorious. Ask citizens what's most urgent, and 26.4% across every religious stripe say the economy — the one thing seculars, traditionalists, and the religious actually agree on. Ask what decides their vote, and the economy drops to fourth place at 9.6%. First place, at 30.9%, is "the leader's personality". So Israelis vote on vibes, then wonder why the cost of living never gets fixed. Democracy, but make it a personality cult. Speaking of cults: Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu polls at 29.2% overall, yet among voters who think the country's on the right track, 60.9% call him the best man for the job — while only 39.5% of that same happy crowd say they'll actually vote Likud. A 21-point gap between loving the king and voting for his party. Somewhere in there is a man Israelis want on the throne but not on the ballot. Meanwhile the two camps can't even agree on what country they live in. 42.8% of secular Israelis insist Israel is basically a religious state; 49.8% of religious Israelis insist it's basically secular. Both sides are convinced they're losing the same war — a fight with no finish line, because nobody's actually counting the same scoreboard. Into this void steps Derech Eretz, running the state's plumbing because the state itself apparently can't read paperwork: the Chief Rabbinate, per the report, still can't process Soviet-era documents, leaving Russian-speaking Israelis waiting years for the kind of marriage and burial approvals other citizens get in a week. The fix on the table — elected neighborhood councils, a Russian-speaking chief rabbi — isn't radical. It's just what happens when volunteers have to build the office the government forgot to open. Two-thirds of Israelis are dissatisfied with their government. Nine in ten have already made up their minds anyway. If that's not a definition of a stuck system, what is?
