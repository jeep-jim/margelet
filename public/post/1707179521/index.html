THE SUPREME COURT JUST ELEVATED THE FEDERAL RESERVE INTO A CLASS OF ITS OWN One of the biggest takeaways from today’s Supreme Court decisions isn’t just that the President now has broader authority to remove the leaders of most independent federal agencies. It’s that the Court simultaneously reaffirmed that the Federal Reserve is different. The Court ruled that the President has broad constitutional authority to remove the heads of most independent agencies because executive power ultimately rests with the President. But when it came to the Federal Reserve, the Court carved out an exception. The justices allowed Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook to remain in office while her legal challenge proceeds, emphasizing the Fed’s unique role and longstanding independence. Think about what that means. For virtually every other executive agency, the Court said presidential control is constitutionally required. For the Federal Reserve, the Court said the opposite principles apply. While the Fed was “created” by Congress under the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, it operates with a level of independence unlike almost any other institution in the federal government. Consider its position: • The President appoints Federal Reserve governors—but cannot simply remove them at will. • Congress created the Federal Reserve and can amend or repeal the law that governs it—but Congress does not direct the Fed’s day-to-day monetary policy or dismiss governors based on policy disagreements. • Courts can review whether the Federal Reserve acts within the law—but they generally do not substitute their own monetary policy judgments for those of the Fed. The practical result is an institution that exercises enormous influence over the nation’s money supply, interest rates, inflation, employment, banking system, and financial markets while operating with an extraordinary degree of insulation from direct political control. It leaves one of the most powerful institutions in America with virtually zero democratic accountability.
