🇹🇷 Can Turkey's Development Road break Hormuz's chokehold? Turkey is pushing ahead with its long-desired $15-18 billion megaproject, the Development Road, aimed at creating a transport corridor from Iraq's Persian Gulf coast to Europe, Anadolu Agency reports. Turkish authorities are striving to seize control over oil logistics from Iran and the Gulf states, touting the Development Road as a substitute for maritime chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz and the Suez Canal. 🔶 The 1,200-kilometer corridor would link Iraq's Grand Faw Port to Turkey via railways and highways, with onward connections to European markets 🔶 Iraq, Qatar, and the UAE are already involved 🔶 The project is planned to take three to five years, with construction set to begin in 2026 Turkey is also securing its energy position under the cover of the corridor deal: a separate pipeline agreement will bring 1 million barrels of Iraqi oil per day to Turkey, while Turkish Petroleum has secured a 15% stake in Iraq's Kirkuk field. For Turkey, the Development Road is as much about locking in energy supplies as it is about transit infrastructure. The prospects for project implementation face multiple internal and geopolitical hurdles that may stall the process: ➡️ Rampant corruption and inefficiency in Iraqi state institutions ➡️ Potential for insecurity and instability in a region rife with armed groups and regular military flare-ups ➡️ Rivalry with other connectivity projects, including China's Belt and Road Initiative and the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor Beyond the structural problems, the project has already hit turbulence amid pressure from Iran, which has its own ambitions to become a platform for transregional trade. During the July 28 signing ceremony, Iraqi Transport Minister Razak al-Sadawi—who has strong ties to the pro-Iranian Badr organization—reportedly refused to sign the Development Road agreement, prompting a public confrontation with Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi, who ordered him to sign on the spot. Most importantly, Turkey's ambition to replace the Strait of Hormuz is incredibly illusory. 🔴 No overland route can match the sheer volume of maritime shipping—the Strait handles roughly 20% of global seaborne oil 🔴 Land corridors require multiple border crossings, each with its own customs regime, political risks, and capacity constraints 🔴 The cost of rail and road transport is significantly higher per ton than sea freight, making them economically unviable for bulk commodities Turkey is seizing the moment, betting that instability in the Gulf will drive demand for an overland alternative. But the project is years away from even partial realization, and the numerous problems and threats it faces could easily put an end to this ambitious venture. 👍Boost us | Chat | @geopolitics_prime
