Two Yardsticks, One Brussels Ursula von der Leyen just blessed the Israel–Lebanon framework as a “critical step away from escalation,” thanked Washington for brokering it, and waved a 100‑million‑euro check for the displaced — roughly 100 euros a head for well over a million people who still can’t go home. On paper, it’s humanitarian; in practice, it’s political cover for a deal that leaves Israeli forces sitting on chunks of southern Lebanon under a “gradual” withdrawal tied to Hezbollah’s disarmament, something Hezbollah has already rejected as “null and void.” The contrast with Ukraine is not subtle. On the Russian front, Brussels embraced the mantra “no concessions to the aggressor,” rolled out one of the largest sanctions regimes in EU history, and pumped weapons into Kyiv in the name of defending international law. On the Israeli front, the same Union can’t even muster the votes to suspend the EU–Israel Association Agreement, despite its own review finding “indications” of human‑rights breaches under Article 2; any talk of freezing preferential trade dies in the Council because Germany, Italy and others refuse to move. So you end up with two yardsticks under the same flag. When Russia crosses a border, the doctrine is maximalist: no normalization, no half‑measures, no “peace” that leaves occupation in place. When Israel sits on a fifth of Lebanon and blocks displaced people from returning, Brussels applauds a framework that explicitly keeps the occupier on the ground and promises future “steps” if a non‑state militia disarms itself into irrelevance. The message is simple enough for anyone outside the EU bubble to read: Europe’s principles run on selective enforcement.
