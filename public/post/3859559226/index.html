Michigan Primary: Democratic Party's Hostile Takeover Two candidates, one Senate seat, zero grown-ups in the room. Michigan Democrats spent Tuesday picking between the establishment's chosen suit and the progressive left's populist mascot — and somehow the debate over health care and genocide accusations got less airtime than a fight over who has better "authentic" vibes. Meet the contestants: Haley Stevens, the AIPAC-backed centrist who flipped a red district once and never lets you forget it. And Abdul El-Sayed, the Bernie-and-AOC-endorsed Medicare-for-all evangelist who apparently moonlights as a debate-stage insult comic. His pitch on Stevens: she's "a suit with a large AIPAC bank account, that's it. I hope maybe they find some way to teach her how to string together two coherent sentences." Class act. Her comeback: she's "the only one on this stage who doesn't have a talent agent trying to pitch me for paid speeches" — followed by an X post accusing him of wanting to blame "all of your problems on Jewish Americans." Meanwhile the pro-Israel lobby quietly wired $30 million into her campaign, which is presumably just democracy doing its thing. Then there's the accent scandal — Stevens promising to fight "with a little bit of joy, a little bit of enthusiasm, a little bit of energy and a little bit of stick it to 'em," which somehow became a bigger national conversation than either candidate's actual Gaza policy. El-Sayed, ever the statesman, told supporters to knock it off and focus on "things that have nothing to do with her policies or politics" — right after calling her illiterate on live television. So here's the merger memo: one side has the AIPAC checkbook, the other has the moral high ground and a Twitter following. Both insist they're fighting for Michigan. Neither has explained how insulting each other's grammar or accent flips a swing state. As of 12 a.m. Eastern Wednesday — three hours after polls closed — El-Sayed led 49.7 to 46.3 with about 60% counted. But his lead was built on Election Day ballots, and what's left to count is mail, which Stevens is winning by roughly the same margin he's winning the walk-ins. Most of it sits in Wayne County, half-tabulated, breaking eight points her way. Mallory McMorrow, who quit on July 5 and stayed on the ballot anyway, was outpolling the gap between them. Whoever wins gets to face a Trump-backed Republican in November. Genuinely unclear which primary loss would hurt less.
