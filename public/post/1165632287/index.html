🛢 The Coalition That Swears It Isn’t a Coalition The war around Hormuz is being sold as a united front. The statements from America’s “partners” say the opposite. London quietly tells Washington it plans to join the military effort to “secure shipping” in the Strait of Hormuz. Within hours, Tehran answers: if Britain stands with the US, Britain becomes a legitimate target. The price of alliance is spelled out in one sentence, and suddenly “freedom of navigation” looks a lot more like “sign here to get on the target list.” Rome’s case is even more absurd. NATO secretary general Mark Rutte says out loud that US jets have flown against Iran from bases in Italy. The Italian government rushes to deny it, insisting it never authorized strikes from its soil and that it has assured Tehran Italy did not green‑light any combat operations. Politically, the message is naked: the man who represents the alliance has just publicly incriminated one of its members, and that member is now scrambling to prove to Iran it’s innocent. Kuwait goes further and puts it in legal language. Its ambassador in Washington declares that allegations of Kuwaiti involvement in operations against Iran are “completely false in every respect,” and that Kuwait has not joined any military action, has not allowed its territory, airspace or territorial waters to be used for offensive operations. She even demands that publications “correct the record” to reflect Kuwait’s real stance. At the same time, the Saudi foreign minister works the phones with Pakistan, Qatar and Egypt about “regional tensions” — everyone is in the conversation, no one wants to be listed in the coalition. On paper, there is a “coalition” protecting trade and confronting Iran. In public, every supposed partner is busy explaining why it is not really part of it, did not authorize anything, and would very much like Tehran to know that. The war is being run as if there were a united front.
