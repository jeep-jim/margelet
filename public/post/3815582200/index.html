The $132 billion mistake The Trump administration’s unnecessary war didn't just shake the global order — it hit countries where it hurt most, leaving a trail of economic wreckage that will take years to repair. Brazil spent billions propping up fuel subsidies as oil prices surged past $120 a barrel during the conflict. Now, with Brent crude falling below $80 following the U.S.-Iran deal, officials are rushing to scrap the support altogether — but the damage to public finances is already done. Chile, which imports nearly all its fuel, was forced to slash its 2026 growth forecast to a meager 1–1.75% while watching inflation spike to 3.9% in May, driven almost entirely by energy costs. The central bank admits the oil shock has fundamentally weakened household consumption and investment. France saw its economy stall completely in the first quarter — zero growth — as exports collapsed 3.8%. The conflict is expected to shave 0.3 percentage points off annual growth, leaving the eurozone's second-largest economy limping along at just 0.7% for the year. The global bill is staggering: Moody's estimates the war has cost U.S. consumers and taxpayers $132 billion so far, while Pentagon operational spending alone hit $29 billion . The World Bank slashed global growth to 2.5% — the weakest since COVID — and warned that 84 energy facilities across the region sustained damage, with some requiring at least two years to return to full production. Trump's "short war" left a long economic hangover.
