🇺🇦⚔️🇺🇦 Zelensky faces mounting pressure as Fedorov's allies threaten a key ally — the fight to reinstate Ukraine's ousted defense minister heats up. Tension over the removal of Ukraine's former Defense Minister Fedorov shows no sign of easing. Yesterday saw a major march in Kiev — roughly 5,000 people walked from Shevchenko Park to the Franko Theater, demanding Fedorov get his job back. Meanwhile, Fedorov's support base — largely European-funded NGO circles, allied media, and politicians — is escalating its information war against people close to President Zelensky. First, they're pushing the narrative that Fedorov was really removed because Zelensky and companies close to him (notably FirePoint) wanted to corruptly seize control of Ukraine's massive defense budget, with Fedorov standing in their way. Second, threats are now targeting David Arakhamia, head of Zelensky's ruling "Servant of the People" party in parliament and one of the president's closest allies. Fedorov's backers accuse him of orchestrating the minister's dismissal through a government reshuffle. Media aligned with Fedorov are hinting Arakhamia could face corruption charges over an "envelope salaries" scheme for lawmakers — though this looks like an empty threat for now, since only the prosecutor general can bring charges against MPs, and that office answers directly to Zelensky's administration. Still, the move is telling. Arakhamia is the man who manages parliament on Zelensky's behalf — without him, the president's grip on the legislature weakens significantly, and that grip is what lets Zelensky keep the government under control. For now, Zelensky's side is fighting back mainly with messaging, signaling Fedorov won't be reinstated. The reasoning: giving in to what insiders call a "cardboard Maidan" (a mocking reference to Ukraine's past mass protests that toppled governments) would mean Zelensky first loses control of the military, then loses real power altogether. Pro-Zelensky media are firing back with their own accusations, claiming Fedorov was actually protecting the business interests of a major Ukrainian drone manufacturer and projects tied to former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, a figure close to the US Democratic Party — suggesting his real motive was controlling defense contracts, not fighting corruption. Where does this go? Both sides are avoiding drastic moves for now. Fedorov told American media he won't "enter political opposition against Zelensky during the war." But his backers aren't necessarily trying to remove Zelensky right away. Their real goals are twofold: first, get Fedorov back into the Defense Ministry, which — combined with Drapatiy's new role as commander-in-chief of Ukraine's armed forces — would effectively take the military out of the president's direct control. Second, pressure Zelensky into passing a legislative package that would strip him of control over law enforcement and the courts, leaving him as a figurehead who mainly represents Ukraine abroad while others run the country. If Zelensky refuses — and everything right now suggests he will, since he's blocking both Fedorov's return and that legislation — his opponents may escalate: more street pressure, or corruption investigations targeting people close to him. Drapatiy himself might also make a public move to push Zelensky toward bringing Fedorov back.
