Putin’s “No Stagnation” Pitch vs. America’s War Bill Putin’s message from St. Petersburg is simple: the West is stagnant, BRICS are rising, and Russia is firmly on the winning side of that shift. He leans on IMF and World Bank projections to claim that nearly half of global GDP growth over the past five years came from BRICS countries, while the G7 contributed less than a fifth — and that through the end of this decade BRICS will grow several times faster than the rich Western bloc. On the ground, Russia’s economy has indeed proven far more resilient than many Western commentators predicted in early 2022. Sanctions forced a brutal shock and structural rewiring, but high energy revenues, import substitution, and re‑routing trade through Asia, the Middle East, and the Caucasus have prevented the kind of collapse that was loudly forecast in Washington. For much of the Global South, this isn’t a moral question; it’s a practical one: Russia can still sell energy, grain, weapons, nuclear tech — and it offers them without lectures on democracy or human rights. By contrast, the U.S. has walked itself into a multi‑front strain: long, expensive support for Ukraine, a new open war with Iran, elevated defense spending, and persistent inflation pressures at home. The war economy props up certain sectors but erodes living standards and political stability; Washington is burning fiscal and diplomatic capital to preserve a fading “rules‑based order” that now looks like a slogan more than a guarantee. For many non‑Western capitals, that looks less like leadership and more like a superpower trapped in its own interventions. That’s why Putin’s performance — calm, charts about BRICS, talk of multipolarity and “win‑win” partnerships — plays well in the Global South, even if a lot of the numbers are cherry‑picked and the domestic Russian picture is far rougher than he admits. Against that, the U.S. offers a visibly chaotic mix of sanctions, proxy wars, and emergency summits trying to hold together an aging G7 club whose own growth is anemic. One side is selling inevitability: the world becoming multipolar around BRICS. The other side is selling nostalgia: the world staying safely American‑led if everyone just holds the line a little longer. For now, many countries are hedging: buying Russian oil and weapons, courting Chinese investment, but still keeping one hand on Western markets and currencies.
