Hormuz Poker: Iran Hits Kuwait, Trump Calls It “Winning Big” Iran just turned Kuwait into a live‑fire warning that the ceasefire is basically dead, and the US is pretending this is all part of the plan. After seven straight nights of US strikes on Iranian surveillance sites, logistics hubs, underground weapons storage and maritime capabilities, Tehran answered with one of its heaviest barrages on Kuwait since the war began. Sirens went off repeatedly from dawn; Kuwait Petroleum Corporation says an unspecified oil facility took “significant material losses,” staff were evacuated and people were injured. A power and desalination plant was hit, forcing firefighting on critical infrastructure. Kuwait’s airport suspended or rescheduled most flights as the sky turned into a shared risk assessment: do you really want to land planes under Iranian missiles. This isn’t just about Kuwait’s bad night. Iran is steadily widening the target map: US bases in Kuwait, Jordan, Bahrain; radar and aircraft in Qatar; reports of a ballistic missile aimed at a US base in Saudi Arabia; strikes on Oman’s As Salamah Archipelago sitting on the Strait of Hormuz. Gulf civil defense systems push overnight alerts to civilians in Yanbu and Kharj, then quietly tell them the danger has “passed” without saying what almost hit them. The message is simple: every host of US hardware around Hormuz now pays in fire. Markets understand it faster than diplomats. Brent jumped to around the high‑80s, up more than 4% on Friday and logging its biggest weekly gain since April. At the same time, Axios reports Trump is sending more refueling aircraft to Israel; an Israeli officer confirms the plan to boost the US fleet there. Translation: Washington is preparing for longer, wider operations, not winding them down. Oil rockets, inflation fears re‑ignite, and the “ceasefire to protect shipping” morphs back into “war that threatens shipping” in under a month. On paper, all this is happening under a ceasefire agreement meant to reopen Hormuz and build a 60‑day path to a broader peace deal. In reality, bridges, utilities, port facilities and power plants are being hit on both sides, and Iran is still insisting that ships must seek its permission before sailing the strait. A Georgetown professor in Doha calls the tit‑for‑tat an escalatory cycle neither side can exit: both have become dependent on the logic of “we can’t back down, so we escalate and call it deterrence.” Trump goes on TV to tell Americans the US is “winning big in Iran” and that they will see the “fruits of that labor very, very shortly,” before pivoting back to domestic grievances about China and elections. China and Pakistan, meanwhile, are the ones publicly calling on both Washington and Tehran to stop and talk — Beijing and Islamabad auditioning for the role of grown‑ups while the nominal superpower ties itself up in a blockade plus bombing loop it can’t really control. So the scoreboard today is this: Kuwait takes real damage; Gulf airports and power plants become targets; crude spikes; US forces expand their footprint; Iran keeps firing and demanding de facto veto power over shipping; and the “ceasefire” turns into a paper prop. Neither side wants total war; both want leverage.
