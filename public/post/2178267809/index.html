Trump at G7: Selling a Deal, Leaving the Fine Print to Tehran Trump arrived at the G7 looking like a tired salesman with one product left: the Iran deal he still can’t put on the table. In Évian, he promised fellow leaders that Hormuz is practically open already, insisting “we have a lot of lanes right now” and calling the strait “open and toll‑free.” At the same time, Iranian outlets like Fars are briefing their public that any “free transit” is strictly time‑limited — 60 days, then Tehran reserves the right to charge, regulate and weaponize the chokepoint it just proved it can close. The president sells permanent relief; the other side advertises a paid trial period. The White House message machine is working overtime to cover that gap. Talking points sent to surrogates stress that Trump “won’t hesitate” to resume strikes if Iran cheats, that “the pressure stays fully loaded,” and that there is “no open‑ended timeline” for Tehran to comply with the memorandum of understanding. That language is clearly aimed at nervous hawks who see the same problem critics do: the text of the deal is still secret, the timelines are fuzzy, and Washington is asking markets and allies to take it on faith that deterrence and verification are more solid than they look. Meanwhile, global indicators are reacting to the headline, not the footnotes. Stocks are climbing, bonds are rallying, oil is slipping on the assumption that Hormuz will really reopen and stay open. Shipowners and insurers are less romantic: they remember previous presidential claims about “safe passage” that did not match what radar screens and risk models were telling them. Until the shooting stops in Lebanon and the rules in the strait are written, they know the true guarantor of security is not a G7 press spray but actuarial math. Inside the summit hall, the subtext is even sharper. European leaders have already been talking about a world “less reliant on the U.S.” for security guarantees; now they are watching a war their ally started, could not finish on its own terms, and is trying to declare over via an interim memo whose nuclear and regional provisions are still undefined. Trump leans on the language of strength and flexibility; Iran leans on the leverage of a proven chokehold. Both sides say they’ve won. The only consensus so far is that no one is ready to show the actual contract.
