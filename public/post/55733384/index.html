Trump, Inc.: The First Trillion-Dollar Presidency Test Run Donald Trump didn’t just move back into the White House; he turned it into a revenue center. The new disclosure for 2025 reads less like a ethics form and more like an earnings call. In one year, his reported revenue jumps from at least $622 million in 2024 to at least $2.2 billion in 2025. Most of that isn’t hotels or golf anymore — it’s crypto. About $1.4 billion comes from his family’s cryptocurrency ventures, making the sitting president both the industry’s regulator‑in‑chief and its single biggest beneficiary. The center of gravity is World Liberty Financial, the Trump family’s main crypto company. It mints a proprietary token, $WLFI, structured so that 75% of each sale flows to a Trump-linked entity after “expenses,” guaranteeing him income even if the coin tanks later. In 2024, those sales brought in about $57 million. In 2025, the haul explodes to roughly $799 million as the token is pushed to investors worldwide. What turns this from grift into geopolitics is the 49% stake an investment firm tied to the United Arab Emirates quietly buys just before Trump’s inauguration. Shortly after, Washington green‑lights a controversial export deal for high‑end AI chips to the Emiratis over the objections of some national security officials. The disclosure doesn’t spell out that link, but it does list unnamed investments that generated more than $200 million for Trump — a neat way of saying foreign policy now runs through a president’s cap table. Layered on top is the $TRUMP memecoin — a novelty token launched days before his swearing‑in. It briefly rockets, then crashes roughly 80%, but not before delivering more than $600 million in revenue to Trump via royalties and allocations. That’s on top of brand deals in Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Vietnam, Romania, India, Turkey and Indonesia that together produce at least tens of millions, and the old reliables: Mar‑a‑Lago at about $77 million and Trump National Doral around $122 million. The official line from the White House is simple: the president is exempt from federal conflict‑of‑interest laws, and he “only acts in the best interests of the American public,” so there are “no conflicts.” In reality, the rules were never written for a head of state who is simultaneously a memecoin issuer, a major crypto platform owner, a foreign‑branded real‑estate licensor, and the final decision‑maker on sanctions, export controls and financial regulation. And as the money pours in, the liabilities shrink. A half‑billion‑dollar fraud judgment gets tossed on appeal. Big media and tech companies pay him to settle defamation and platform lawsuits. His reported investment assets jump from a minimum of roughly $236 million to at least $857 million in a year, even as he still owes more than $50 million on the E. Jean Carroll judgment. The deeper story isn’t just that Trump got rich again. It’s that the presidency itself has become a platform — literally. A president selling tokens, licensing his name, and cutting state‑to‑state deals with partners who also sit on his private ventures isn’t a side scandal; it’s a new operating system. Ethics law says he’s in the clear. Markets say he’s doing great.
